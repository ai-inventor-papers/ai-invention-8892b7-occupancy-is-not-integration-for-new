Host vocabulary predicts whether new scientific concepts take root across disciplinary boundaries

When a scientific concept first appears in a new field, do the words around it at the moment of arrival predict whether anyone in that field will pick it up? This study tracked 426 emerging concepts and 462,812 works across physics, computer science, and biomedicine. The share of a concept's initial partner terms belonging to the host field's own vocabulary predicts five-year newcomer uptake. On an independent biomedical replication of 191 MeSH concepts, each standard deviation of host-vocabulary share raises newcomer uptake by 23% (incidence-rate ratio 1.23, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.36).

Contributions

Host-entry grafting predicts uptake

A pre-registered host-entry test showing that the host-vocabulary composition of a concept's initial partners predicts newcomer uptake, replicated on an independent MeSH biomedical population (co-primary IRR 1.23, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.36; verdict: REPLICATED). Development-set estimates concordant: Screen IRR 1.30, Held-out IRR 1.19.

Outcome-blind concept dataset

A semantically grounded, outcome-blind dataset of 426 emerging concepts with 462,812 OpenAlex works and an independent 191-concept MeSH biomedical check population.

Host-specific, dual-margin effect

The host-vocabulary effect is host-specific (though the Balassa-index lift leg is near-collinear with the continuous share) and operates through both the extensive and intensive margins, with no detectable variation by origin field.

Persistent-neighbour closure predicts emergence

General closure fails on the Held-out fold while the panel coefficient for persistent-neighbour closure is significant (−1.07, Holm p = 0.015; development-set estimate), and the effect is not Burt brokerage.

Diffusion typology and adopter enrichment

A stable two-type diffusion typology (localised vs. broad), an expansion-before-diffusion ordering, adopter-level enrichment for prior partner exposure (OR 3.09; prevalence 0.79 vs. 0.61), and four representative cases.

Method

The study proceeds in four stages (Figure 1). An outcome-blind pool of 426 emerging scientific concepts was assembled from OpenAlex titles and abstracts (first appearance 2005–2016, 20–300 papers in the first-appearance window), split into a Screen fold (247) and a Held-out fold (119). An independent biomedical arm of 191 MeSH concepts was drawn from new MeSH descriptors. The combined corpus comprises 462,812 unique OpenAlex works. A logistic-regression classifier (F1 = 0.82, AUC = 0.89) and a variant merger (B-cubed F1 = 0.78) grounded every concept. The pool is dominated by physics and computer-science concepts from arXiv.

From 259,716 design-weighted background works, 25 yearly three-year-window co-occurrence snapshots were built (approximately 27,000 nodes and 84,000 edges per snapshot), with association-strength edge weights and Leiden community detection.

RQ1: Emergence precursors. For each concept showing sustained uptake (at least 20 papers per year plus at least 20 percentile points in co-word network strength), a matched control was drawn from the same first-appearance band and origin field. The standardised mean difference in closure measures between treated and control concepts at lags −3 to 0 is the event-study estimand. Four closure variants were computed: general, turnover-residualised, persistent-neighbour (restricted to top-20 neighbours present in consecutive years), and Burt constraint.

RQ2: Host-entry grafting. A host-entry event is the first year a concept appears in a non-origin subfield with at least five co-occurring partner terms. Host-vocabulary share is the tag-weighted mean of each partner's pre-entry publication share in the host subfield. The outcome is five-year newcomer uptake: the count of papers by author-disjoint newcomers. A Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood (PPML) regression with concept-clustered standard errors tests whether host-vocabulary share predicts uptake. Two specifications were pre-registered: a primary (concept-by-year and host-by-year fixed effects) and a co-primary (concept, entry-year, and host fixed effects), the latter declared for use when the primary drops below 30 concept clusters.

Results

Structural precursors of emergence

On the Held-out fold (a development-set estimate; the fold was used in pipeline development), only persistent-neighbour closure survives Holm correction: the panel coefficient is −1.07 (Holm p = 0.015) and the matched event-study standardised mean difference is −0.83 (95% CI [−1.71, −0.10]). General closure is not significant (Holm p = 0.147), and neither is turnover-residualised closure (Holm p = 0.635). Burt constraint has a positive sign (+0.11, 95% CI [0.02, 0.19]), indicating that emerging concepts sit in more constrained, not more brokered, ego networks. The picture is of open, fast-renewing connections among a concept's most strongly associated terms within a dense wider ego network.

The MeSH closure replication is directional under a sensitivity label (D = −0.42, Holm p = 0.039, n = 29) but null under the primary label (D = −0.09, Holm p = 0.677, n = 15). Structural precursors add no predictive value beyond frequency, burst, degree and entropy baselines (logistic AUC 0.89 vs. 0.88).

On the independent MeSH biomedical population, a one-standard-deviation increase in host-vocabulary share raises newcomer uptake by 23% (co-primary IRR 1.23, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.36, p < 0.001; primary IRR 1.32, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.60; verdict: REPLICATED). Development-set estimates on the main-arm folds are concordant: Screen co-primary IRR 1.30 (95% CI 1.16 to 1.45), Held-out co-primary IRR 1.19 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.33, wild-cluster bootstrap p = 0.012, nativeness-permutation p = 0.026). The inverse-variance weighted pooled estimate is 1.26 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.36, I2 = 0). Co-transfer of origin companions is null across all folds and specifications.

The fully saturated primary specification on the Held-out fold is inconclusive (IRR 0.98, 95% CI 0.70 to 1.37) because it retains only 250 of 972 events with 30 concept clusters; this was declared underpowered in advance (minimum detectable effect IRR 1.30).

Mechanism decomposition

The host-vocabulary effect operates through both margins: the extensive-margin effect is +6.4 percentage points per SD (95% CI 4.8 to 8.1, approximately a 13% increase relative to the base rate of 51%), the intensive-margin IRR is 1.18 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.27), and the extensive margin accounts for 38% of the total effect (95% CI 21% to 55%). Adding generality controls (subfield entropy and log frequency of entry partners) retains 97% of the host-vocabulary coefficient (inverse-variance weighted retention 0.97, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.10). The Wald test for origin-field dependence is null (p = 0.64).

At the adopter level, authors who take up a concept in a new field are enriched for prior exposure to its entry partners (conditional logistic regression OR 3.09; prevalence 0.79 vs. 0.61; 95% CI 2.32 to 4.28; 1,013 matched strata, 422 entries, 109 concepts; Screen fold, not independently confirmed). The breadth-prediction screen (does pre-emergence openness predict how many new subfields a concept will reach?) found no effect beyond growth and level baselines.

Limitations

Main-arm folds used in development
The Screen and Held-out folds were both used during pipeline development: event counts, population sizes, and gating decisions were computed on both folds before grafting outcomes were read. Estimates on both are development-set results, not true held-out confirmations. The independent evidence for the host-vocabulary effect comes from the MeSH replication.
Population bias
The concept pool is dominated by physics, physical-sciences, and computer-science concepts from arXiv. The MeSH population covers only biomedicine-to-biomedicine entries. Generalisation to social sciences, humanities, or engineering is untested.
Primary specification underpowered
The fully saturated specification with concept-by-year and host-by-year fixed effects is inconclusive on the Held-out fold (IRR 0.98, 30 clusters). The MeSH replication and the development-set estimates rest on the co-primary specification, which leaves more residual variation.
Single-paper entries
87% of co-primary Screen events involve a single entry-year paper. The pooled multi-team effect is positive (IRR 1.28, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.48), but the Held-out fold alone is underpowered for this test.
Binary establishment null
The host-vocabulary share predicts newcomer count and probability of any uptake, but binary establishment (at least five newcomer papers in at least three of five years) is null on the Held-out fold (p = 0.16). The effect is graded, not threshold-based.
Cluster-robust inference
The cluster-robust variance estimator rejects 10–11% of null shuffles in simulation (target 5%). Wild-cluster bootstrap and permutation tests give consistent results (Held-out wild p = 0.012, permutation p = 0.026), but standard p-values should not be taken at face value.
Host-specificity caveat
The Balassa-index lift measure used to test host-specificity has a within-fixed-effect correlation of 0.997 with the log of the continuous host-vocabulary share. The lift test confirms the continuous measure rather than providing independent evidence.
Adopter evidence not independently confirmed
The adopter-level enrichment (OR 3.09) is Screen-fold only. The design cannot separate absorptive capacity from topical proximity.
Closure caveats
The Held-out fold was used in pipeline development, so the closure result is a development-set estimate. The matching falls back to Screen controls for 17 of 25 unique controls, so the estimator is not fully control-side independent. The MeSH closure result is directional under the sensitivity label but null under the primary label.
