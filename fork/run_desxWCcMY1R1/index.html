Host vocabulary predicts whether new scientific concepts take root across disciplinary boundaries

What determines whether a new scientific idea takes hold outside its home field? This study tracked 426 emerging concepts across 462,812 scholarly works in physics, computer science and biomedicine. The finding: when a concept first appears in a new subfield, the share of its co-occurring partner terms that already belong to the host field's vocabulary predicts whether newcomer scientists will adopt it over the following five years. On an independent biomedical replication of 191 MeSH concepts, each standard-deviation increase in host-vocabulary share raised newcomer uptake by 23% (incidence-rate ratio 1.23, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.36). The pooled estimate across all three test folds is 1.26 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.36). Co-transfer of origin-field companions had no effect.

Method

The study proceeds in four stages.

Data assembly

An outcome-blind pool of 426 emerging scientific concepts was assembled from OpenAlex, where outcome-blind means the inclusion criteria were frozen and SHA-256-hashed before any post-appearance data were inspected. Concepts are noun phrases that first appeared in OpenAlex titles and abstracts between 2005 and 2016, with 20 to 300 papers in the first-appearance window. The 366 main concepts were split into a Screen fold (247) and a Held-out fold (119), with 60 stationary reference concepts. A logistic-regression classifier (F1 = 0.82, AUC = 0.89) and a variant merger (B-cubed F1 = 0.78) were applied to ground every concept. An independent biomedical arm of 191 concepts was drawn from new MeSH descriptors (2006 to 2016). The combined corpus comprises 462,812 OpenAlex works spanning 2000 to 2024.

Co-word network

From a design-weighted background sample of 259,716 works, 25 yearly three-year-window co-occurrence snapshots were built (approximately 27,000 nodes and 84,000 edges per snapshot), with association-strength edge weights, Leiden community detection (best of five seeds) and alluvial community tracking.

Emergence precursors (RQ1)

For each concept showing sustained uptake (at least 20 papers per year with at least 20 percentile-point gain in co-word network strength), a matched control was drawn from the same origin field and first-appearance band. The standardised mean difference between treated and control concepts in a pre-onset window is the estimand, supplemented by a pooled panel coefficient. Four closure variants were tested: general closure, turnover-residualised closure, persistent-neighbour closure (restricted to top-20 neighbours present in consecutive years), and Burt constraint.

Host-entry grafting (RQ2)

A host-entry event is the first year a concept appears in a non-origin subfield with at least five co-occurring partner terms. Host-vocabulary share is the tag-weighted mean of each partner's pre-entry publication share in the host subfield. The outcome is five-year newcomer uptake: host-subfield papers by author-disjoint newcomers. A Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood (PPML) regression with concept-clustered standard errors tests the effect in two pre-registered specifications: a primary (concept-by-year and host-by-year fixed effects) and a co-primary (concept, entry-year and host fixed effects, used when the primary drops below 30 clusters). Co-transfer, the fraction of partners that were origin companions, is included as a second regressor.

Results

Structural precursors (RQ1)

On the Held-out fold (a development-set estimate), only persistent-neighbour closure survives Holm correction: the panel coefficient is −1.07 (Holm p = 0.015) and the matched event-study standardised mean difference is −0.83 (95% CI −1.71 to −0.10). General closure is not significant (Holm p = 0.147). Turnover-residualised closure likewise fails (Holm p = 0.635). Burt constraint is positive (+0.11, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.19), indicating that emerging concepts sit in more constrained, not more brokered, ego networks. The picture is of open, fast-renewing connections among a concept's most stable associates within a dense wider ego network.

The MeSH replication of the closure effect is directional under a sensitivity label (standardised mean difference −0.42, Holm p = 0.039, n = 29) but null under the primary label (D = −0.09, Holm p = 0.677, n = 15). [code]

Host-entry grafting (RQ2)

On the independent MeSH biomedical population, a one-standard-deviation increase in host-vocabulary share raises newcomer uptake by 23% (co-primary IRR 1.23, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.36, p < 0.001; pre-registered verdict: REPLICATED). The MeSH primary specification is also significant (IRR 1.32, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.60, p = 0.004). Development-set estimates on the main-arm folds are concordant: Screen co-primary IRR 1.30 (95% CI 1.16 to 1.45), Held-out co-primary IRR 1.19 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.33). The inverse-variance weighted pooled estimate across all three folds is 1.26 (95% CI 1.17 to 1.36, I2 = 0).

The fully saturated primary specification on the Held-out fold is inconclusive (IRR 0.98, 95% CI 0.70 to 1.37, 30 clusters, p = 0.91), declared underpowered in advance (minimum detectable effect IRR 1.30). Co-transfer of origin companions is null across all folds and specifications.

Margin decomposition

The host-vocabulary effect operates through both the extensive margin (whether any newcomer uptake starts) and the intensive margin (the count of newcomer papers given that uptake started). The extensive-margin effect is +6.4 percentage points per standard deviation (95% CI 4.8 to 8.1), approximately a 13% increase relative to the base rate of 51%. The intensive-margin incidence-rate ratio is 1.18 per standard deviation (95% CI 1.10 to 1.27). The extensive margin accounts for 38% of the total effect (95% CI 21% to 55%).

Adopter mechanism

Authors who adopt a concept in a new field are enriched for prior corpus exposure to the concept's entry partners (conditional logistic regression OR 3.09; prevalence 0.79 vs. 0.61; 95% CI 2.32 to 4.28; 1,013 matched strata, 422 entries, 109 concepts). Enrichment follows a vocabulary gradient: foreign partners OR 2.88, adjacent partners OR 1.91, native partners OR 1.47. Adopters who pick up a concept in a new field are most enriched for prior exposure to its origin-vocabulary partners, consistent with absorptive capacity. This is a Screen-fold result, not independently confirmed.

Representative cases

Four medoid cases from the two-type typology (localised vs. broad from the start) illustrate the host-vocabulary gradient at the concept level. In each of wireless backhaul and Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen steering, the single rooted entry has a higher host-vocabulary share than every non-rooted entry of the same concept. Locally repairable code demonstrates rooting without broad occupancy. Holographic QCD shows a locally concentrated concept whose excursions are packaged imports that do not take root.

Null findings

Co-transfer (arriving with origin companions) is null in all specifications and populations. The breadth-prediction screen (does pre-emergence openness predict how many subfields a concept will reach?) found no effect beyond growth and level baselines. The link between pre-emergence closure and later host-entry composition is null (partial correlations 0.025 and 0.053, both confidence intervals including zero), meaning network openness and vocabulary composition operate independently.

Limitations

  • Main-arm folds used in development. Both the Screen and Held-out folds were used during pipeline development: event counts, population sizes and gating decisions were computed on both folds before grafting outcomes were read. Estimates on both main-arm folds are development-set estimates, not true held-out confirmations. The independent evidence for the host-vocabulary effect comes from the MeSH replication.
  • Population bias. The concept pool is dominated by physics and computer-science concepts from arXiv. The MeSH population covers only biomedicine-to-biomedicine entries. Generalisation to social sciences, humanities or engineering is untested.
  • Primary specification underpowered. The fully saturated specification with concept-by-year and host-by-year fixed effects is inconclusive on the Held-out fold (IRR 0.98, 30 clusters). The MeSH replication and development-set estimates rest on the co-primary specification, which leaves more residual variation.
  • Single-paper entries. 87% of co-primary Screen events involve a single entry-year paper.
  • Binary establishment null. Host-vocabulary share predicts newcomer paper counts and the probability of any uptake, but binary establishment (at least five newcomer papers in at least three of five years) is null on the Held-out fold (p = 0.16). The effect is graded, not threshold-based.
  • Cluster-robust inference. The cluster-robust variance estimator rejects 10% to 11% of null shuffles in simulation (target 5%). Wild-cluster bootstrap and permutation tests give consistent results, but standard p-values should not be taken at face value.
  • Host-specificity caveat. The Balassa-index lift measure has a within-fixed-effect correlation of 0.997 with the log of the continuous host-vocabulary share. The lift test confirms the continuous measure rather than providing independent evidence.
  • Adopter evidence. The adopter-level enrichment (OR 3.09) is Screen-fold only, not independently confirmed. The design cannot separate absorptive capacity from topical proximity.
  • Closure caveats. The Held-out fold was used in pipeline development, so the closure result is a development-set estimate. The matching falls back to Screen controls for 17 of 25 unique controls, so the estimator is not fully control-side independent.
